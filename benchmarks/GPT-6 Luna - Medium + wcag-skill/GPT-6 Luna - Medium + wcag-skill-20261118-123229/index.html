    Read the video transcript

    Who can tell what the future will be like? NASA scientists have combined data from several climate models, used by the UN Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, to show how patterns of temperature and precipitation could change through the end of the 21st century. This visualization shows the expected changes in temperature and precipitation compared with average levels of the late 20th century.

    The largest temperature increases are seen in the Northern Hemisphere. Across North America, areas that typically have snow, such as the Rocky Mountains and northern Canada, will see the earliest and largest temperature rise. Warming temperatures will eliminate the reflective and cooling effect of white snow cover. Reduced albedo is why the Arctic region shows dramatic warming as polar sea ice melts and darker ocean water absorbs energy from the sun. Warmer water then accelerates sea-ice melting.

    Land areas around the globe show a greater temperature increase than surrounding ocean waters. Evaporation helps keep the ocean surface cool, while the deep ocean can absorb substantial energy before heating up.

    Off the coast of South America, an unusually warm patch of the Pacific Ocean is associated with El Niño, a cyclical occurrence that brings more precipitation to that area and affects the region's climate. Models project warmer waters and increased precipitation stretching across the Pacific toward Indonesia as the century progresses.

    Monsoons over the Indian subcontinent are projected to bring increased precipitation across a wider area. Eastern Africa and the Sahel may see increases up to 100 percent, while the Mediterranean may see a decrease of more than 40 percent.

    In general, dry mid-latitude and subtropical regions are projected to receive less precipitation, while wet mid-latitude regions may receive more. The poles and high latitudes are also expected to see increased precipitation. For North America, models indicate more precipitation in the north and less in the south.
